# Storyline

Everything on this site is one argument, made seven times. This file is where I
keep the whole thing straight: the story I picked, the actual myths underneath
it, what each section is doing, and why each interaction is the shape it is.

---

## The story I chose

The obvious version of a Shiva and Parvati love story is the wedding. Divine
couple, cosmic marriage, garlands. I did not want that one.

I wanted the version that comes before the wedding, because that is the part
that is actually about love rather than about celebration:

**He lost her. He did not move on. She came back and did the work of reaching
him. Neither of them was whole apart, and what they end up as is not a couple,
it is one body.**

That is the arc, and it maps onto seven phases of the moon:

```text
●      ◖       ◐            ◑         ◒       ◓         ○
void   grief   the union    devotion  sight   promises  fullness
```

The first three sections are the myth. The last four turn toward her. That shift
is deliberate. The myth earns the right to be personal, and then it gets out of
the way. By the time I am promising her anything in my own voice, she has already
read what devotion cost the two of them, so the promises land on something.

### Why this myth and not another

Because my name is already in it. **Sivan is the Tamil form of Shiva.** I did not
pick the theme to be clever, it was there before I started. The site never says
this out loud anywhere. She can notice it or not.

## The myths I actually used

I keep these separate from the design notes so I know which parts are the
tradition and which parts are me. Where I bent something, I say so.

### Sati, and the ash

Sati, also called Dakshayani, is Daksha's daughter. She marries Shiva against her
father's will. Daksha holds a great sacrifice, deliberately does not invite
Shiva, and insults him publicly. Sati goes anyway, and when she hears what her
father says about her husband she throws herself into the sacrificial fire. This
is where the word *sati* comes from.

Shiva's response is not grief as anyone would recognise it. He tears out a lock
of his hair and creates Virabhadra, who destroys the sacrifice and beheads
Daksha. Then he takes Sati's body and will not put it down. In the version I like
most, Vishnu has to follow him and cut the body away piece by piece with his
discus to make him stop, and wherever a piece lands becomes a Shakti Peetha, a
place of pilgrimage. There are fifty one of them, depending who is counting.
Grief, distributed across a subcontinent.

Afterwards Shiva goes to the cremation grounds and smears himself in ash.

**Where I bent it:** the ash Shiva wears is cremation ash, *bhasma* or *vibhuti*,
and traditionally it means the body is temporary and he is beyond it. It is not
canonically *her* ash. Tying it directly to Sati is my move, and it is the
biggest liberty I have taken anywhere on this site. I kept it because it is true
to how grief actually behaves. You do not wear a symbol of impermanence. You wear
the person.

Used in: **Bhasma**, and the sixth promise.

### Parvati, and the waiting

Sati is reborn as Parvati, daughter of Himavan, who is the Himalaya himself, and
Mena. She decides as a girl that she will have Shiva, who by this point is a
widower sitting in the snow with his eyes shut.

She does not wait to be noticed. She goes up the mountain and performs *tapas*,
which gets translated as austerity but literally means heat. Standing on one leg.
Through summer, through monsoon, through winter. Eating less and less until she
is eating nothing at all, which earns her the name **Aparna**, "she who did not
take even a leaf". Kalidasa's *Kumarasambhava* is the great telling of it and it
does not flinch: her beauty goes, her body goes, and she keeps going.

Shiva tests her. He comes down disguised as an old ascetic and slanders himself
to her face, listing everything wrong with the man she is waiting for. She
defends him to a stranger without knowing it is him. That is when he gives in.

The gods also send Kama, desire itself, to shortcut all this by making Shiva fall
in love. Shiva opens his third eye and burns him to ash on the spot. Desire does
not get to force this. She has to earn it, and so does he.

Used in: **Tapasya**, and every line about waiting.

### Ardhanarishvara

The composite form. One body, split down the middle: Shiva on the right, Parvati
on the left. Matted hair, tiger skin and trident on one side, soft features,
ornament and a mirror on the other. Her being the left side matters in the
iconography. *Vama*, left, is the feminine side.

There are a few origin stories. The one I built the section around is Bhringi. He
was a sage so devoted to Shiva that he refused to acknowledge Parvati at all, and
would circle only Shiva in worship, never the two of them together. So Parvati
merged with her husband, and Bhringi found there was no longer any way to go
around one without going around the other.

That is the entire mechanic of that section. You cannot bring one half back
without bringing the other.

In another version, Brahma's creatures cannot reproduce, creation has stalled,
and Shiva appears in this form to show that the feminine is not decorative but
structural. Nothing continues without it.

Used in: **Ardhanarishvara**, the seam, the ash and gold sides of the page, and
the fifth promise.

### The third eye

Famous for burning. It burned Kama, and in other stories it burns the world at
the end of a cycle. But its meaning is *jnana*, knowledge. It is the eye that
sees what is actually there rather than what is in front of it.

There is a smaller, domestic story I like more than the burning ones. Parvati
comes up behind Shiva and playfully covers his two eyes with her hands, and the
whole universe goes dark, because his eyes are the sun and the moon. So he opens
the third one to keep the light on while she plays.

**Where I bent it:** my section says the third eye is not for burning, which is a
straight inversion of the most famous story about it. That is on purpose. The
tradition says it destroys illusion. I am saying that paying real attention to
someone is the same act.

Used in: **Trinetra**, and the glass.

### The moon

Shiva wears a crescent on his head and is called Chandrashekhara, moon crowned.
Chandra, the moon, was cursed by Daksha to waste away, and Shiva took him onto
his forehead and preserved him at the moment before he vanished. The moon on
Shiva is something that was dying and was kept.

The moon travels through twenty seven **nakshatras**, lunar mansions, one a
night. Rohini is the one he loves best, and he lingers there.

**Amavasya** is the new moon, the dark night, associated with ancestors and with
what has not begun yet. **Purnima** is the full moon, the night nothing is
hidden.

**Where I bent it:** there are fifteen or sixteen tithis in a fortnight, and
twenty seven nakshatras. My seven phases are not a set anyone would recognise. It
is a sequence I designed that borrows the vocabulary.

Used in: the whole structure, the moons across the bottom of the screen, and the
first and last section names.

### One older thing

The first line of the site is standing on the Nasadiya Sukta, Rig Veda 10.129,
the creation hymn. It says that in the beginning there was neither existence nor
non existence, and that what arose first was *kama*, desire.

Desire is older than light. That is not my idea, it is about three thousand years
old, and the first section is a paraphrase of it:

> Before there was light, there was the wanting of it.

## The seven sections

For each one: what it is, which myth it comes from, what the interaction means,
and what I am actually saying.

### ● Amavasya, "Before Light"

**The myth:** the Nasadiya Sukta, desire before creation. And Amavasya itself,
the dark of the moon, before anything.

**On screen:** total black. One point of light, breathing. She holds still near
it and it catches, and the poem opens out of it.

**The symbolism:** the interaction is the argument. Nothing arrives until she
stops moving and pays attention, which is what the whole site asks of her, so it
may as well be the first thing it teaches. The seam does not exist yet, because
there is nothing to divide yet.

> Before there was light, there was the wanting of it.
> Before the first star, someone was already looking up.

Hidden line: *You were the reason I started paying attention.*

### ◖ Bhasma, "The Ash Years"

**The myth:** Sati's death, and what he did afterwards.

**On screen:** the verse is buried under ash. She has to wipe it off with her
finger to read it.

**The symbolism:** grief is a physical thing sitting between you and what you
loved, and it does not lift on its own. You have to put your hands in it. The
verse being unreadable until she works for it is not a puzzle for the sake of
one. It is the only honest way to present three thousand years of mourning.

> When he lost her, he did not build a shrine.
> He wore her as ash across his shoulders and called it clothing.
> Three thousand years of that, and not one of them spent forgetting.
> I understand him better than I would like to admit.

That last line is the hinge of the whole site. It is the first moment the poem
stops describing a god and admits it is about me. Everything afterwards is
allowed to be personal because of it.

Hidden line: *Grief is just love with nowhere to go. Mine had somewhere.*

### ◐ Ardhanarishvara, "One Body, Divided"

**The myth:** Bhringi, and the composite form.

**On screen:** one line of verse torn in half, the halves pulled apart. The far
half is blurred and dimmed so she cannot read the end of it. Dragging either half
moves both, and when they meet they dissolve into a single line.

**The symbolism:** three things at once.

Dragging one half moves both, because that is what Parvati did to Bhringi. There
is no arrangement of this where one of us moves alone.

The far half is unreadable until she closes the distance, because the point of
the section is that neither half means anything by itself. She earns the end of
the sentence:

> half of me · was always walking toward you

And the seam is **born here**. It does not exist in the first three sections. The
moment she closes the gap, light bursts out of the middle of the screen and the
seam grows to the full height of the page, gold, and stays there to the end. It
is not scenery. It is the thing she made, and she can see it in every section
after this one.

> They are not two who met and stayed.
> They are one body that spent an age learning its own left side.
> I have stopped pretending I was ever whole on my own.

Hidden line: *Not two halves of a couple. One being, remembering itself.*

### ◑ Tapasya, "She Did the Waiting"

**The myth:** Parvati on the mountain. Aparna. The disguised ascetic.

**On screen:** one small flame, guttering. The verse writes itself only while she
keeps her hand near it. Move away and the flame drops and the words stop mid
sentence, with the cursor blinking, waiting for her.

**The symbolism:** this is the section I was most careful with, because the easy
version of it makes her passive and the myth does not. Parvati is the one doing
the work. She is not waiting to be chosen, she is on a mountain making it happen
while he sits there with his eyes closed.

So the mechanic asks her to stay. Not to solve anything, not to be clever, just
to be still and present while something slowly comes into being. That is what
tapas is, and it is the only interaction on the site that costs her nothing but
patience.

> She did not wait to be chosen.
> She stood in the snow until the snow gave up,
> and kept one flame alive for a hundred years,
> until even a god had to open his eyes and look.

The turn toward her is held back to the hidden line so it arrives later and
quieter: *You did the waiting. I have not forgotten what it cost you.*

### ◒ Trinetra, "The Third Eye"

**The myth:** the third eye as knowledge rather than fire. Her hands over his
eyes.

**On screen:** she gets a piece of glass she can drag around. Lines invisible to
the naked eye can be read through it. Holding it over this section's own hidden
line opens the eye.

**The symbolism:** every section before this one has had a line hidden in it
since the day I wrote it. This is where she finds out, and the glass stays with
her for the rest of the visit, so she can go back and sweep everything she has
already read.

That is the most romantic idea in the build and it is not mine, it is what the
third eye actually means. There was always more there than she could see, and the
seeing is the discipline. Love as sustained attention rather than a feeling that
happens to you.

> The third eye is not for burning.
> It is for looking at one thing long enough to see what it actually is.
> I have been looking at you all this time and I am nowhere near finished.

Hidden line, and the one that sends her back up the page:
*Every part you have already read is hiding something. Go back.*

### ◓ Nakshatra, "The Stars I Follow to You"

**The myth:** the moon's path through the twenty seven nakshatras, one a night,
and Rohini, the one it lingers at.

**On screen:** a slow field of stars, six of them brighter, joined by a faint
line into one shape. Each of the six is a promise. Nothing to solve.

**The symbolism:** the moon does not wander. It moves through named stars in an
order, every night, and it always ends up in the same places. Promises are the
same kind of thing. Fixed points you navigate by, not feelings you have.

Two of the six deliberately answer earlier sections, which is why this one sits
sixth and not second:

> If the worst ever comes, I will carry you the way he carried her. Not as grief.
> As clothing.

answers Bhasma, and

> Your name goes beside mine in everything. Never after it.

answers Ardhanarishvara. Neither means much to someone who has not read the myth
yet. She has.

There is nothing to solve here on purpose. Five gated sections in a row would
turn a gift into a task, so this one just opens.

Hidden line: *I did not choose these because they were the easy ones to promise.*

### ○ Purnima, "Everything I Wrote Down"

**The myth:** the full moon. Nothing hidden. The crescent he saved, all the way
back.

**On screen:** letters drifting in the dark as folded paper with wax seals. She
can push them around, and opening one gives it the whole screen.

**The symbolism:** the site has spent six sections withholding things. Verses
under ash, half a sentence out of reach, lines that need glass to read. This
section withholds nothing. It is the full moon, everything is lit, and the last
thing she does here is read plainly.

She does not write back through the site, and that is deliberate. The last thing
she reads is unambiguously mine. A text box at the end would have turned a letter
into a form.

Hidden line: *I wrote all of these before I knew whether I would send any.*

## How the design carries the story

None of this is decoration. Each piece is doing a job.

**The seam.** A vertical line down the middle of the page, ash tinted on the
left, gold on the right, which is the Ardhanarishvara split as the iconography
has it. It does not exist until she joins the halves, and then it never leaves.

**The colours.** Six, and each one is from the myth.

- *Bhasma* ash grey, what he wore.
- *Moon* bone white, the crescent he saved.
- *Datura* violet. Datura is genuinely sacred to Shiva and genuinely poisonous. A
  flower you can die from, offered in devotion. That is the register of this whole
  site in one plant, so it is the accent colour.
- *Gold* for Parvati. She is called **Gauri**, which means golden, shining.
- *Kumkum* red, used about six times on the entire site. Bindi, wax seals, the
  third eye.
- *Void* near black with a violet bias, for the dark before anything.

**The moons along the bottom.** The progress display and the structure at the
same time. Never numbers, because the moon already encodes it: the dark fills in
as she goes, and the last one is full.

**The backdrop.** It darkens as she solves more, so by the end the poem is the
only lit thing on the screen.

**The Devanagari.** One word at the foot of each section, very low opacity, in
the same typeface as the English so they read as one voice rather than two fonts.
It is texture, not content, and it is marked so screen readers do not try to
pronounce ornament.

## Rules for the writing

- **No em dashes.** They read as machine written, which is fatal here.
- **Every section needs a hidden line.** It costs one line of copy and it is the
  most personal writing on the site, because it is the part she has to go looking
  for.
- Second person, but sparingly. Most sections talk about the myth and let the
  last line turn toward her.
- No exclamation marks, nothing arch, no emoji.
- The Hindu material gets done properly or not at all. If I am not sure whether
  something reads as devotional or as costume, I ask someone who would know.
- Anything I have actually written to her beats anything invented for this.

## The one moment this is all for

Everything above is scaffolding for the second she reads *half of me was always
walking toward you*, the two halves click together under her thumb, and the seam
bursts open down the middle of the page.

Protect that moment when changing anything.
